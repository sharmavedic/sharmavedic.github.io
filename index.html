The Problem

A tragedy of the commons

In 1968, Garrett Hardin described a shared pasture where every herder, acting purely in their own interest, adds one more animal to graze. Individual rationality ruins the collective well-being as the pasture is overgrazed and the resource collapses for everyone.

How real communities avoid it

Real-life communities regularly avoid this fate. Political economist Elinor Ostrom showed that, left to self-govern, groups sharing a resource develop mechanisms like local monitoring and graduated social consequences — no privatization or central authority required. I ask: can the same kind of self-governance be built into artificial agents, purely through how we shape their incentives?

Building it into agents

My work introduces an additional reward mechanism called social reward. This, alongside personal utility, lets a group of self-interested agents organically produce a leader, which steers the collective behavior toward socially optimal outcomes, avoiding the tragedy.

How the simulation works

Four shepherds share one 6×6 pasture. Every tile holds a grass level from 0 (bare) to 5 (lush) — the six shades in the legend above the grid. Each shepherd owns cows, and every cow wears its shepherd's colour. Cows are turned out on random tiles with energy.

With 3-4 cows each, the pasture eventually dies and no food remains for the herd — the tragedy playing out in real time. With 1-2 cows, each shepherd can enjoy the pasture's benefits indefinitely.

Every time step, each cow acts once

It either eats the tile it is standing on or walks to one of its neighbours (at most four, fewer at the edges). The choice is a softmax over the value of the two actions: 

f       = grass ÷ 5                 fertility, 0 to 1

P(eat)  = 1 / (1 + e^((Q(move) − Q(eat)) / ))

So a nearly empty cow eats almost whatever it is standing on, while a well-fed one walks past all but the best grass. On a bare tile P(eat) is zero and the cow always moves. Eating takes one level off the tile and returns energy.

When it moves, the direction is drawn with weight e  × f for each neighbour, so greener ground pulls harder without ever being a sure thing. A cow only ever sees the tiles it could step onto — nothing here knows the state of the pasture as a whole.

Then, at the end of the step

Every cow spends energy whatever it did, so each one needs a mouthful roughly every steps to break even. Coats pale as energy runs down, and a cow that reaches zero has starved — it fades off the pasture and is gone for good.

Each tile then regrows one level with probability for every one of its eight neighbours still holding level or above. That caps at for an interior tile, along an edge and in a corner, which have fewer neighbours to seed them.

Why it collapses

Grass only spreads from grass. Graze a patch flat and there is nothing left to reseed it, so the pasture loses the ability to heal in exactly the places it has been hit hardest. Demand rises with the herd while regrowth is capped by how much green is still standing: at one cow per shepherd the pasture holds indefinitely, at three it is stripped and the whole herd starves within a few hundred steps.

Project 01 · Extended Abstract, AAMAS 2026

Low Message-Cost Gossip for Welfare-Optimal Decentralized Decision Making

with Prof. Peter Marbach · Paphos, Cyprus

Now take those herders and multiply them. Shepherds across the whole region graze their own pastures, and years of experience have taught each of them roughly what a pasture can carry — one cow is safe but wasteful, four strips it bare. Their views differ because their perspective differs: some prioritize gains over higher chance of stripping pasture, while others are more cautious prioritizing long term pasture health over potential gains. No central authority collects those opinions and averages them. But the shepherds do talk to each other.

Sharing a favourite is enough

The naive fix is to have every agent broadcast its full ranking — but that gets expensive fast as the number of options grows. This work shows you don't need to: a gossip protocol where each agent shares only its current favorite option (or top few) is enough to provably converge on the option that maximizes total welfare across the group — even if that option starts out as nobody's favorite, a scenario we call option neglect.

More choices, less chatter

The most surprising part: in settings where one option is a clear standout — a common pattern in swarm coordination and decentralized marketplaces — the protocol needs less communication as the number of options grows. The shepherds are one instance; the same structure turns up wherever agents hold private opinions and no server is there to poll them.

Gossiping shepherds

Shepherds converge on the best option (two cows each)

How the simulation works

Twenty shepherds, each from their own pasture, drift around the box. Every one of them has spent years learning what a pasture can carry and holds a private utility for each herd size — one, two, three or four cows. Nobody can see anyone else's numbers, and there is no central authority to collect them.

Each shepherd keeps an estimate per herd size, starting at its own private utility, and is coloured by whichever estimate is currently highest. That is the option it would name if asked today, and it changes as the estimates move.

When two of them interact

They walk in continuous time but only look around every  s. At each check, the closest two shepherds who are not on cooldown and are within units of each other meet. Each names the single option it currently rates highest, and both replace their estimate of that option with the average of the two: 

i names option a,  j names option b

est_i(a) = est_j(a) = ( est_i(a) + est_j(a) ) / 2
est_i(b) = est_j(b) = ( est_i(b) + est_j(b) ) / 2

The exchange runs both ways, so one interaction can move two options; if they happen to name the same one it is a single average. Both then go on cooldown for about  s, varied by so cooldowns do not expire in lockstep — with identical timers the population locks into fixed couples who have nothing left to tell each other.

Why averaging finds the best answer

Pairwise averaging is a consensus operation: it conserves the total, so an option's estimate drifts toward the population mean utility for that option — which ranks the options in exactly the order total welfare does. Crucially, the option a shepherd keeps naming is the one being dragged toward the truth, while options nobody mentions sit untouched at whatever that shepherd privately thought.

The two populations

Mixed views gives everyone the same broad shape — more cows is better until the pasture dries up — plus the noise of local experience. Three cows leads the vote at the start even though two cows takes the welfare sum.

Option neglect splits them into camps that genuinely disagree: eight swear by one cow, eight by three, four by four. Two cows is everybody's second choice and nobody's first, so it starts with zero support — yet it still maximises welfare. It gets found because the popular answers deflate toward their true means until the neglected one floats to the top.

When it stops

The run ends when the lowest estimate anyone holds for two cows rises above the highest estimate anyone holds for anything else. An average always lands between its two inputs, so once those ranges are disjoint no further interaction can bring them back together — the result cannot be overturned.

Project 02 · Pending Review

Learning Norms Through Emulation and Prestige

Three reward types, one welfare-optimal norm — without a single vote

Shepherds don't only care about their own cows. This project gives every agent three possible reward types, only one of which is active at a time. Personal utility is the ordinary payoff of one's own behavior. Emulation rewards copying someone else's behavior, in proportion to that behavior's reputation — how much welfare it generates for the group. Prestige rewards the agent being copied, in proportion to the total benefit its behavior provides everyone now following it.

How the norm emerges

The three steps in the animation (see right) sketch the idea. At first every agent is in personal-utility mode, each choosing whatever behavior serves it best on its own (Step 1). One behavior turns out to generate more welfare than the rest, so its reputation climbs, and the other agents realize copying it pays better than going it alone — they switch to emulation mode and adopt it (Step 2). The agent being copied then switches too: now in prestige mode, its reward depends on how much its behavior benefits its followers, so it starts choosing whichever behavior helps them most (Step 3). That is exactly the welfare-optimal norm — and because everyone else is still emulating it, the whole group converges on it too.

Why gossip alone is not enough

Project 01 has shepherds gossip their way to a single best option, but running that directly on norms breaks down in two ways. It asks every agent to rank all possible norms and know a utility for each, and the space of norms is large. And when several norms sit close to welfare-optimal, the protocol struggles to settle on one of them — agreement fails not because the group is wrong but because it cannot break a near-tie. Emulation and prestige sidestep both problems: no agent ever ranks the full space of norms, and there is no tie to break — whoever is currently most beneficial simply attracts the most followers.

The three reward types

Every agent can earn a reward in one of three ways, but only one at a time — an agent is always in exactly one mode, and its mode is what determines which reward it is chasing.

Personal utility

The ordinary payoff of one's own behavior — how many cows it can graze, how well it eats. This is the only reward on offer at the very start.

Reputation

A property of a behavior, not of any one agent: how much welfare that behavior generates for the group. Some behaviors are simply better for everyone than others, and reputation is how good a behavior actually is.

Emulation

The reward for copying someone else's behavior, in emulation mode. It is directly proportional to that behavior's reputation — copying something highly reputed pays better than copying something mediocre, or than sticking with personal utility.

Prestige

The reward for being copied, in prestige mode. It is a multiple of the total benefit an agent's behavior provides everyone currently following it — more followers, or a more beneficial behavior, both raise it.

Step 1 — personal utility

Every agent starts in personal-utility mode, each independently choosing whatever behavior serves it best. Nobody is watching anybody else yet.

Step 2 — emulation

One behavior turns out to generate more welfare than the rest, so its reputation climbs. The other agents notice that copying it would pay better than their own personal utility, switch to emulation mode, and adopt it.

Step 3 — prestige

The agent being copied switches mode too. In prestige mode its reward depends on how much its behavior benefits its followers, so it starts choosing whichever behavior helps them most — which is exactly the welfare-optimal norm. Because everyone else is still emulating it, that norm then reaches the whole group.

Project 03 · In progress

The Dynamics Behind the Norm

Personal utility alone fails; a leader everyone else emulates does not

Project 02 shows the idea works. This project runs the realistic version: agents keep running estimates of their rewards and reputations, behaviors are parameterized rather than picked from a short list, and every agent has only local, limited information. Underneath, an agent is always in one of two modes — acting on a behavior of its own, or participating in someone else's — and the three simulations below are where that distinction actually does the work Project 02 only sketched.

All three pastures below run on the same seed and starting grass, so any difference between them comes from the herd-size policy alone. Greedy fixes every shepherd at its own personal-utility-maximizing herd size — the top pick of the first four shepherds from Project 01. Together that overgrazes the pasture; nobody destocks, and once a shepherd's herd has starved out it simply waits for the grass to come back and turns its cows out again. Fixed is Project 02's baseline: two cows, every shepherd, always. Leader starts identically to Greedy, but one shepherd plays an adaptive rule from the start — three cows once the pasture reads above 70% grass, two otherwise — and the other three switch, one by one, to mirror whatever that shepherd is currently doing. The chart tracks cumulative grass eaten across all three, the same metric Project 02 used.

How the three simulations work

All three pastures use exactly the grazing model from The Problem above, started from the same grass and the same random seed, so nothing but the herd-size policy differs between them.

Greedy

Shepherds one through four each run their own personal-utility-maximizing herd size — cows respectively. These are the top picks of the first four shepherds in Project 01's utility table — nobody is coordinating, everybody is doing what's individually best for them. Together that is more stock than the pasture can carry, so the grass goes and the cows starve.

Nobody ever destocks by choice. But once a shepherd has lost its whole herd and the empty pasture starts growing back, it turns its cows out again — the smallest herd first, the largest last, each waiting for the pasture to look good enough to it: grass. Each of them is still doing the individually sensible thing, and the pasture just cycles.

Every shepherd is fixed at cows for the whole run — Project 02's baseline norm.
